Why landing at the lunar south pole is hard

The Sun never climbs. At the pole it stays within 1.6° of the horizon all year; 160 km away (where IM-2 landed) it never rises above 6.8°. It circles around the sky once a month instead of crossing it.

So hills decide everything. A ridge a few hundred metres high can hide that low Sun for days. But a lander on a peak can see the Sun even when it is “below the horizon”, because from up high the horizon drops.

Earth wobbles. Earth hangs almost still in the lunar sky, but it bobs up and down by about ±7° every four weeks (libration). Near the pole it disappears below the horizon for up to 15 days — no direct radio link home.

The date matters as much as the place. Sunlight follows a 29.5-day cycle and Earth a 27-day one, so the times when both are up drift from month to month. This tool checks every landing date in 2027–2028.
